*A letter to myself, and to whoever needs to understand — not a legal document, just the truth as I lived it.*

---

I got married on 28 July 2010. From that day we lived in the center of Moscow — first on Rochdel'skaya street, a few steps from the White House, with the river right there under our windows. Later we moved to Baumanskaya street, still right in the historic center. We were never far from the "expensive" part of town. That was our life then.

By that point I had already built something of my own — a garment retail business, from literally nothing, up to 8 physical shops and somewhere between €1 and €1.5 million a year in revenue. It wasn't handed to me. I built it.

In August 2017 we moved to Firenze as a family. Not because I wanted to — I actually didn't want to move to Italy, precisely because I knew my "baby-project," my company, wouldn't survive without me physically there running it. But we moved anyway.

For the first year I kept flying back and forth between Firenze and Moscow. Partly it was the visa regime — I still held a long-term visa and had to stay within the 180/180-day rule. But mostly it was because I still cared, deeply, about the business I had built. I applied for my residence permit on the grounds of family reunification somewhere in 2019–2020, and by 2020 I had stopped travelling internationally altogether. But that year of back-and-forth in between — that was the beginning of the end for me, in more than one way.

Eugene found it hard to manage the household even with the nanny there. It became nearly impossible whenever she needed to leave. The kids were still so little, they needed me constantly, not a mother who reappears every few weeks. So after a year of tearing myself apart between two continents, I made the decision — I closed the biggest part of the shops. I didn't want to keep flying away from my family for business trips.

For a while I kept a small piece of it alive — two shops. But retail at that scale simply cannot survive without the founder physically present. I tried anyway, for over two years, watching it slowly fade: the company was opened in 2010, we moved in 2017, by 2021 it was barely breathing, and in 2022 it was officially closed and delisted.

And then, at some point, my daughter ended up in the hospital with a life-threatening illness. Needless to say, everything else stopped mattering. I was with her 24/7. Whatever tiny bits of my professional life were still hanging on — I let them die. There was no other choice, and honestly, I wouldn't have made a different one.

So yes — all in all, I sacrificed my career, and practically my own sense of purpose, the project I had cared about so much, for my family. I knew this would happen before we even left Moscow. That's exactly why I didn't want to move.

Moving my savings out afterward wasn't simple either — SWIFT was blocked between Russia and Europe, so at some point crypto rails were the only option left. And I lived off those savings. I never once asked my husband for financial help. He covered the rent — in the last months in Firenze, then in Lerici, then in Genova. But everything else — the children's activities, their day-to-day life, everything a mother does — that was on me, from my own money.

Eventually, my money ran out. I had "eaten it up," as I put it to myself. And while I was scraping by, the tension between me and my husband kept growing. He bought himself a brand-new BMW X5 — I heard estimates of €100,000 to €120,000 — instead of keeping the one I had given him for his birthday back in Moscow. He bought an IWC watch, something like €22,000–23,000. He went on trips to premium hotels, ate at expensive restaurants — for himself. Meanwhile I was driving an old, worn-out Mercedes that had been through years of family trips and business errands (he used it too, for hauling his own work equipment around Europe, a car that was never even formally assigned to him) — while I was the one figuring out, every single month, how to cover the fundamentals: food, clothes, the kids' tournaments, driving them across different cities in Italy for competitions, paying for school and sports club events as they came up.

While he worked full time and traveled constantly, I was fully focused on our children and our household. My tiny, unfocused attempts to restart something professional were doomed from the start — and in a small, conservative, rather cut-off place like Lerici, with no direct train connection, I don't think any "fashion genius" could dust themselves off and rebuild a business from scratch, not really. I understood that with my mind. But with my heart, I felt what he was really saying — that this was somehow my own fault, that I was "too emotional."

At some point his lack of empathy turned into something closer to real cruelty, and I started trying to leave. We talked about separation, on and off, over and over. Every time I felt sure of my decision, he would talk me out of it. We even went to family therapy at one point. It didn't take us very far — there still wasn't much moral or financial support coming from his side, just a lot of criticism. He eventually didn't want to continue the sessions; he started blaming the therapist herself, accusing her of pushing me toward wanting a divorce — when really, I think I became more convinced of that because of how he behaved even during the sessions. Aggressive, even there. At one point he stormed out on the therapist entirely. And the therapist herself confirmed, afterward, that what she'd witnessed from him was cruelty.

The dissatisfaction on both sides reached its limit. So from my side — I am declaring that living under one roof is no longer possible. I am asking to live with my children, with their father seeing them as is normally arranged, and I won't stand in the way of that. But I am not, right now, in a position to cover all the running costs of life for my children and myself alone. So I have to ask him for a financial contribution — enough to maintain a decent standard of living and let the kids keep doing their social and sports activities, with me there to bring them.
